Deeper Evidence Layer

Peer-Reviewed Research Expansion

This page extends HFAF beyond institutional guidance into peer-reviewed research on automation bias, cognitive offloading, metacognition, critical thinking, and human–AI collaboration.

Scholarly safeguard: evidence that aligns with HFAF does not automatically validate the framework as a whole. Each source is used only for the claims it actually supports.

Automation bias and over-reliance

Romeo & Conti — AI & Society

A systematic review of 35 peer-reviewed studies on automation bias in human–AI collaboration. The review identifies factors such as trust calibration, AI literacy, expertise, verification demands, explanation complexity, and active user engagement.

HFAF relevance: strengthens the case for Discernment and independent verification.

Metacognition in GenAI learning

Xu et al. — British Journal of Educational Technology

A 2025 quasi-experiment with 68 college students examined explicit metacognitive support in a generative-AI learning environment.

HFAF relevance: supports reflective, self-regulated AI use rather than passive consumption.

Open DOI →

Fan et al. — British Journal of Educational Technology

Examines the possibility of “metacognitive laziness” when generative AI reduces the cognitive effort learners would otherwise exercise themselves.

HFAF relevance: informs the distinction between useful augmentation and substitution of the very cognitive process a learner needs to practice.

Cognitive offloading

Iqbal et al. — Scientific Reports

Reports relationships among generative-AI use, cognitive offloading, shared metacognition, and academic achievement among preservice teachers.

HFAF relevance: cognitive offloading should not automatically be treated as harmful. The question is what is delegated and what capacity the human still needs to retain.

Open DOI →

Roy & Jony — Journal of Interdisciplinary Studies in Education

A qualitative study of 12 university students reported perceptions of superficial engagement, lower self-regulation of cognitive effort, and growing reliance on AI-supported thinking.

HFAF relevance: adds qualitative evidence for risks when AI bypasses meaningful learning. The small contextual sample should not be generalized broadly.

Human–AI collaborative decision-making

Technology in Society — Human and Generative AI Interaction

A quasi-experimental study found potential benefits of generative AI for data-heavy and unfamiliar decision situations while also identifying over-reliance and contextual-creativity limits.

HFAF relevance: supports complementary division of cognitive labor rather than simple replacement.

Emerging synthesis

  1. AI can augment performance and reduce cognitive burden.
  2. Cognitive offloading is not inherently harmful; its consequences depend on what is delegated and what capability must be retained.
  3. Automation bias and over-reliance are documented risks.
  4. Metacognitive support, active verification, and meaningful engagement appear important for preserving human participation.
  5. The appropriate human–AI division of labor varies by task, expertise, risk, and learning objective.
