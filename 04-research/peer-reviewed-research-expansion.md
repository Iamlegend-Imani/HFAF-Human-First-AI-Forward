# Peer-Reviewed Research Expansion

This layer extends Human First, AI Forward beyond institutional guidance into peer-reviewed research. It is intentionally separated from the project's conceptual propositions so that evidence can be traced to the claims it actually supports.

## 1. Automation bias and over-reliance

### Romeo, G., & Conti, D. (2025). Exploring automation bias in human–AI collaboration: a review and implications for explainable AI. *AI & Society*.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02422-7

**What the research establishes:** This systematic review synthesizes 35 peer-reviewed studies on automation bias and over-reliance in human–AI collaboration. It identifies trust calibration, AI literacy, professional expertise, cognitive profile, verification demands, explanation complexity, and user engagement as relevant factors. The review argues that explanations alone are often insufficient to prevent over-reliance and that active verification and critical engagement are important intervention points.

**HFAF relevance:** Supports the importance of the Discernment stage and the requirement that AI output remain subject to independent human verification. It does not by itself validate the full Human–AI Agency Loop.

## 2. Metacognitive support in generative-AI learning environments

### Xu, X., Qiao, L., Cheng, N., Liu, H., & Zhao, W. (2025). Enhancing self-regulated learning and learning experience in generative AI environments: The critical role of metacognitive support. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*, 1842–1863.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13599

**What the research establishes:** In a quasi-experiment with 68 college students, explicit metacognitive support was examined as a way to strengthen self-regulated learning in a generative-AI environment.

**HFAF relevance:** Supports designing AI-mediated learning so that students are prompted to reflect, monitor, and regulate their own cognition rather than merely consume AI outputs.

---

## 3. Metacognitive laziness and learning

### Fan, Y. et al. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology*.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544

**What the research establishes:** Examines how generative AI affects learning motivation, processes, and performance and raises the concern that learners may reduce metacognitive effort when AI support becomes readily available.

**HFAF relevance:** Directly informs the distinction between augmentation and substitution. AI use can be beneficial while still creating risks if the technology displaces the cognitive process the learner is meant to practice.

---

## 4. Cognitive offloading and shared metacognition

### Iqbal, J., Hashmi, Z. F., Asghar, M. Z., & Abid, M. N. (2025). Generative AI tool use enhances academic achievement in sustainable education through shared metacognition and cognitive offloading among preservice teachers. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 16610.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-01676-x

**What the research establishes:** Reports relationships among generative-AI tool use, cognitive offloading, shared metacognition, and academic achievement among preservice teachers.

**HFAF relevance:** Important because cognitive offloading is not inherently negative. HFAF should distinguish productive delegation from cognitive substitution rather than assuming all offloading represents deskilling.

---

## 5. Students' experiences of cognitive offloading and critical thinking

### Roy, B. K., & Jony, M. S. (2026). Generative AI and critical thinking in higher education: Students’ narratives of cognitive offloading. *Journal of Interdisciplinary Studies in Education*.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.32674/jey59q25

**What the research establishes:** A qualitative phenomenological study with 12 university students reported perceptions of superficial engagement, reduced self-regulation of cognitive effort, increasing reliance on AI-supported thinking, and awareness of cognitive risks.

**HFAF relevance:** Adds qualitative evidence for the concern that poorly designed AI use can bypass meaningful engagement. The small qualitative sample should not be generalized beyond the study context.

---

## 6. Human–GenAI collaborative decision-making

### Exploring collaborative decision-making: A quasi-experimental study of human and Generative AI interaction. (2024). *Technology in Society, 78*, 102662.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techsoc.2024.102662

**What the research establishes:** A quasi-experimental study across organizational decision scenarios found potential benefits of generative AI in unfamiliar and information-heavy situations, including reduced cognitive burden and some heuristic bias, while also identifying risks of over-reliance and limitations in contextual creativity.

**HFAF relevance:** Supports a complementary rather than replacement model of human–AI work: AI may be especially useful for data-intensive cognition while humans remain important for contextual judgment, creativity, and accountability.

## Emerging synthesis for HFAF

The peer-reviewed evidence reviewed so far suggests a more nuanced position than either unrestricted adoption or blanket rejection:

1. **AI can augment human performance and reduce cognitive burden.**
2. **Cognitive offloading is not automatically harmful; its effect depends on what is delegated and what capability the human still needs to develop or retain.**
3. **Over-reliance and automation bias are real risks in human–AI collaboration.**
4. **Metacognitive prompting, verification effort, and active human engagement appear important for maintaining meaningful human participation.**
5. **The appropriate human–AI division of labor varies by task, expertise, risk, and learning objective.**

These findings are consistent with several HFAF design principles, but they should not be presented as empirical validation of the HFAF framework as a whole.
